Brand voice facts are the most immediately impactful category you can add to Company Brain. They are what make the difference between AI output that sounds like every other company and content that sounds unmistakably like yours. Once your tone guidelines, vocabulary rules, and communication preferences are Live as Company Facts, every AI tool your team uses — from drafting customer emails to writing documentation to generating social copy — will produce output that reflects your brand without any extra prompting.

## What to Capture

A complete brand voice profile in Company Brain typically covers six elements. You don't need to encode all of them on day one, but the more thoroughly you define your voice, the more consistent your AI output becomes.

- **Overall tone** — the personality your writing should convey. Examples: professional, warm and conversational, technically precise, empathetic and direct, confident but not boastful. Describe it in specific terms your AI tools can act on, not just adjectives.
- **Vocabulary to use and avoid** — the specific words and phrases that are on-brand and off-brand for your company. "Clients" vs. "customers", "invest" vs. "spend", "solution" (avoid) vs. a specific product name (use).
- **How to address customers** — first name or last, formal or informal, "you and your team" or "your organisation". Define the default and any context-specific exceptions.
- **Sentence length and complexity** — whether you favour short punchy sentences, longer flowing prose, or a mix. Whether you use contractions, bullet points, em-dashes, or passive constructions.
- **Common phrases and taglines** — recurring expressions your team uses that signal brand fluency. If you always say "let's get into it" or "here's the short version:", encode those.
- **What NOT to do** — explicit prohibitions are often more useful than positive guidance. "Never use passive voice in customer-facing emails" or "Never open a sentence with 'Please note that'" gives the AI clear guardrails.

## Example Brand Voice Facts

<CodeGroup>
  ```text Tone of Voice
  Title:    Tone of Voice — Core Principle
  Category: Brand & Communication
  Content:  Our tone is professional but approachable. We write the way
            a knowledgeable colleague would talk to you — clear, direct,
            and without unnecessary formality. We use contractions ("we're",
            "you'll", "it's") to stay conversational. We are confident
            without being arrogant, and we never talk down to the reader.
  ```

  ```text Vocabulary Rules
  Title:    Vocabulary — Use and Avoid
  Category: Brand & Communication
  Content:  USE: "client" (not "customer"), "invest" (not "spend" or
            "cost"), "we" and "you" (not "the company" or "the user"),
            our product names exactly as written.
            AVOID: "synergy", "leverage" (as a verb), "best-in-class",
            "world-class", "seamless", "robust", "cutting-edge",
            "disruptive", "innovative". These words are vague and
            overused. Replace them with specific, evidence-backed claims.
  ```

  ```text Customer Address Style
  Title:    How to Address Clients in Writing
  Category: Brand & Communication
  Content:  Address clients by their first name in all emails and messages.
            In formal documents (proposals, contracts, reports), use the
            company name on first reference and "you" and "your" thereafter.
            Never use "Dear Sir/Madam" or "To Whom It May Concern" — find
            the right contact name or use a warm, direct opener like
            "Hi [First Name]," or "Hello [First Name],".
  ```

  ```text Sentence Structure
  Title:    Sentence Length and Structure
  Category: Brand & Communication
  Content:  Favour short, clear sentences. Aim for an average sentence
            length of 15–20 words in emails and external communications.
            Use one idea per sentence. Avoid nested clauses. In long-form
            content (reports, documentation), slightly longer sentences
            are acceptable, but never sacrifice clarity for sophistication.
            When in doubt, split the sentence in two.
  ```
</CodeGroup>

## Before and After

Here's what a difference brand voice facts make in practice. Both examples use the same AI prompt: *"Write a follow-up email to a client after a demo call."*

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Without Company Brain">
    The AI produces a serviceable but generic draft that could have come from any company:

    ```text
    Subject: Following Up on Our Demo

    Dear [Name],

    Thank you for taking the time to attend our product demonstration today.
    We hope you found it informative and that it provided a clear overview
    of our solution's capabilities.

    Please do not hesitate to reach out should you have any questions or
    require further information. We would be happy to schedule a follow-up
    call at your convenience.

    Best regards,
    [Your Name]
    ```

    This email is polite but impersonal, passive, and forgettable. Phrases like "do not hesitate to reach out" and "at your convenience" are filler. The tone doesn't reflect your company at all.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="With Company Brain">
    With brand voice facts loaded, the same prompt produces a draft that sounds like your team actually wrote it:

    ```text
    Subject: Great talking through it — here's what's next

    Hi Sarah,

    Really enjoyed walking you through Company Brain today — your questions
    about the MCP integration were spot on, and I think the demo answered
    most of them.

    Here's what I'd suggest as next steps:
    - I'll send over the technical spec doc by end of day Thursday
    - We book a 30-minute call with your CTO to cover the security questions
    - You share this with your team and gather any initial reactions

    Does that work for you? Happy to adjust the timeline if Thursday is tight.

    Talk soon,
    Alex
    ```

    The AI used first names, active voice, short sentences, a concrete next-steps list, and the conversational directness encoded in your brand voice facts — without any extra prompting.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Testing Your Voice Facts

Once you've written your first set of brand voice facts, run through this quick validation process to make sure they're working as intended.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Add 3–5 brand voice facts">
    Start with your tone principle, your vocabulary rules, and your customer address style. These three facts cover the most common failure modes in AI-generated content. Submit them for Curator review and get them to Live status.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Ask your AI tool to write a sample email">
    Open your MCP-connected AI tool and give it a realistic writing task: "Write a follow-up email to a client after a discovery call" or "Write a short intro paragraph for our new feature announcement." Don't add any style instructions — let Company Brain do the work.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review the output against your brand">
    Read the AI's draft and ask: does this sound like us? Check for vocabulary violations (did it use any of your avoid words?), tone consistency (is it warm/formal/direct in the right way?), and address style (did it use the right greeting?). Note anything that still feels off.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Refine the facts based on what's still off">
    If the output uses passive voice too often, add an explicit prohibition fact. If the tone is still too formal, make your tone fact more specific with examples. Each iteration of this loop produces noticeably better output. Most teams get to a consistent, on-brand result within two or three rounds.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
  Your brand voice facts double as an onboarding resource. If you've written them clearly enough for an AI to produce on-brand content, they're clear enough for a new hire to understand your brand in their first week. Share your Brand & Communication facts with new team members as a lightweight brand guide — no separate deck required.
</Tip>
